# Portfolio Case Study Outline

## 1. Project framing

**Title:** AI Economy 2026: Relative AI Exposure, Skills, and Employment Projections

**Research question:** Why do some occupations with high relative AI exposure have positive employment projections while others have negative projections?

## 2. Data and scope

- 831 detailed U.S. occupations.
- BLS AI exposure categories, employment projections, and skills data.
- Projection period: 2025–2035.
- Unit: occupation, not worker or company.

## 3. Analytical approach

- Separate AI exposure from projected employment growth.
- Compare outcomes in the High/Very high relative-exposure group.
- Compare selected occupational skill scores between projected-growth and projected-decline groups.
- Report descriptive differences and correlations with clear caveats.

## 4. Key evidence

- 412 occupations are High/Very high relative AI exposure.
- 306 of 412 are projected to grow; 100 decline; 6 are essentially flat.
- The largest mean skill difference is Science (+0.623).
- The strongest correlation with projected growth is Critical & analytical thinking (0.508).

## 5. Visuals to show

- Exposure-category distribution.
- Projected outcomes among High/Very high relative-exposure occupations.
- Skill-difference bars.
- Correlation dot plot.
- One contrast card: Data scientists (+34.6%) and Data entry keyers (−25.5%), both Very high relative exposure.

## 6. What it demonstrates

- Research framing and question refinement.
- Data joining, validation, exploratory analysis, and evidence communication.
- Responsible interpretation of public labor-market data.

## 7. Limitations and next step

State that the evidence is descriptive, occupation-level, and specific to the 2025–2035 projection cycle. A next phase could test sensitivity to occupation family, industry, education, wage, and local labor-market conditions without turning the work into an AI-risk ranking.
