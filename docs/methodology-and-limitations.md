# Methodology and Limitations

## Research design

The analysis links three BLS products at the detailed-occupation level: 2025–2035 employment projections, BLS AI exposure categories, and BLS occupational skills data. The analytical population is 831 detailed U.S. occupations.

The project first describes the distribution of BLS relative AI exposure categories. It then isolates the 412 occupations classified as High or Very high relative exposure and compares projected employment outcomes from 2025 to 2035. Finally, it compares selected skill scores between the projected-growth and projected-decline groups and calculates descriptive correlations between skill scores and projected employment growth.

## Definitions that matter

- **Relative AI exposure:** BLS’s four-category classification of how occupations compare with one another based on theoretical and observed AI exposure measures.
- **Projected employment growth:** BLS’s projected percentage change in employment from 2025 to 2035.
- **Growing / declining:** A descriptive grouping based on the direction of the projected percentage change.
- **0% projected change:** A exactly zero projected change; it is retained in headline counts but excluded from the binary growing-versus-declining comparison.

## Interpretation rules

This package does not use the following claims:

- that AI exposure is a probability of worker replacement, automation, job loss, adoption, wage change, or productivity change;
- that occupations should be sorted into a career-protection ranking;
- that a skill causes projected employment growth;
- that the results predict an individual’s career outcome.

The central result is an observed pattern in BLS data: high relative AI exposure and projected employment decline do not move together one-for-one.

## Limitations

1. **Projection uncertainty.** Employment projections are estimates based on BLS methodology and assumptions. They are not guaranteed outcomes.
2. **Relative measure.** The BLS exposure category is relative to the occupations and input sources in the BLS classification. It does not express an absolute level of exposure.
3. **No causal identification.** Skill differences and correlations may reflect education, industry composition, regulation, demographics, task mix, technology adoption, occupation size, or other omitted factors.
4. **Occupation-level evidence.** The analysis concerns occupations, not individual workers, employers, firms, or local labor markets.
5. **Aggregation.** Four-category exposure labels simplify heterogeneous tasks within each occupation.
6. **Time specificity.** All findings in this package use the 2025–2035 BLS projection cycle and should not be mixed with older or newer cycles without rerunning the analysis.
7. **Examples are illustrative.** Named occupations explain the contrast; they are not rankings, recommendations, or a complete list of opportunities or risks.

## Source method notes

BLS builds its AI exposure categories from five external sources, summarized into theoretical and current-evidence dimensions and clustered into four relative categories. BLS explicitly states that exposure does not imply job loss, productivity gains, automation probability, or wage effects. See the [BLS AI exposure methodology and limitations](https://www.bls.gov/emp/publications/ai-exposure-categories.htm).

Skills are BLS occupational measures based on its Employment Projections skills framework. See [BLS Skills Data](https://www.bls.gov/emp/data/skills-data.htm).
